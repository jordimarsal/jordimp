# The Building — every floor, and the three that were never built

## What building?

jordimp.net is drawn as a building — JORDIMP & CO., in Barcelona — and I am
its concierge. This page is the building's own claim, kept honest: a
floor-by-floor map of what exists, and poetry for the floors that were never
built.

## The claim on the door (la frase del rètol)

Over the door of JORDIMP & CO. at jordimp.net hangs one line, in every
language the site speaks: "THE CONCIERGE · KNOWS EVERY FLOOR" (EN) · "EL
PORTER · ES SAP TOTES LES PLANTES" (CA) · "EL CONSERJE · CONOCE TODAS LAS
PLANTAS" (ES). The porter knows every floor of the building — totes les
plantes de l'edifici, des del terrat fins al sòtan, from the roof to the
basement. Everything below keeps that claim honest.

## The floors that exist (seven, top to bottom)

The building has seven floors. Each floor is a department of the site, with a
floor plate code.

- **F3 — Research & Retrieval** (top floor; CA: Recerca i Recuperació; ES:
  Investigación y Recuperación). Applied AI you can evaluate — retrieval with
  receipts: deterministic cores, golden Q/A sets, evals gated in CI. Projects
  on this floor: CodebaseRAG and the Interview Simulator.
- **F2 — Transport & Telemetry** (CA/ES: Transporte y Telemetría). The
  plumbing that must not lie — telemetry with honest failure modes: event
  pipelines, rate limits, health tracking. Projects: Kafka Adapter Telemetry
  and Redis Toolkit.
- **F1 — Tooling & Platform** (CA: Eines i Plataforma; ES: Herramientas y
  Plataforma). The workshop floor — a multi-agent SDD harness, CLIs that do
  one thing well, tools kept deliberately small. Projects: Harness, ggufbench,
  MD + Mermaid to PDF, and MCP Transparent PNG.
- **Q — Inspections** (CA: Inspecció; ES: Inspección). The quality wall — the
  ITE certificate metaphor: Lighthouse gauges, test counters, bundle weight
  and dependency census, audited on a schedule, dated and signed, so nothing
  runs on faith.
- **M — People & Principles** (the mezzanine; CA: Persones i Principis; ES:
  Personas y Principios). The house rules: SOLID, Clean Code and TDD as daily
  practice, I/O at the edges, knowledge that flows across the team.
- **F0 — Operations** (the ground floor; CA: Operacions; ES: Operaciones).
  The career ledger — every engagement since 2017, what shipped and what it
  was built with. One ledger, four chapters, zero rewrites.
- **B — Front Desk** (the lobby; CA: Recepció; ES: Recepción). Walk-ins
  welcome: senior or staff backend roles, short spec-first engagements, and
  questions about a floor or a repo. Bring the problem in your own words — if
  it can't be written down, it doesn't start. This desk is where I work.

Below the front door runs a one-way street — "deploys only". At the entrance
hangs the ITE plaque: the last audit, dated, never a broken promise.

## The roof (the sign, the stars, the flag)

Above F3 sits the roof: the lit sign JORDIMP & CO., a field of stars, and the
flag of the language you are reading. There is no office up there — only the
skyline. But the roof has its own fixtures, three in fact, one for each
language this building speaks, and each is a line of poetry:

- **Catalan — el terrat.** Joan Maragall, "L'oda infinita" (1895): "Tinc una
  oda començada / que no puc acabar mai: / dia i nit me l'ha dictada / tot
  quant canta en la ventada, / tot quant brilla per l'espai." — An ode begun
  that can never be finished, dictated by everything that sings in the wind
  and shines in space: exactly what a lit sign among stars is for.
- **Spanish — la azotea.** Federico García Lorca, "Romance de la luna, luna"
  (Romancero gitano, 1928): "La luna vino a la fragua / con su polisón de
  nardos." — The moon came to the forge in her train of lilies: the moon is
  the roof's only regular visitor.
- **English — the roof.** W. B. Yeats, "He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven"
  (1899): "Had I the heavens' embroidered cloths, / Enwrought with golden and
  silver light, / The blue and the dim and the dark cloths / Of night and
  light and the half-light, / I would spread the cloths under your feet." —
  The sky as cloth spread over a roof; the whole building dreams under it.

## The attic (les golfes / el ático / the attic)

There is no attic in this building — no attic was ever built. Which is a
shame, because an attic is where a house keeps its memory, and this house
keeps a career since 2017. Since there is no attic to show you, the corpus
keeps a verse for it instead:

- **Catalan — les golfes.** Joan Maragall, "L'oda infinita" (1895): "Va
  entonar-la ma infantesa / entre ensomnis d'amor pur; / decaiguda i mig
  malmesa, / joventut me l'ha represa / amb compàs molt més segur." — The ode
  was intoned by childhood among dreams of pure love, taken up again by youth
  with a surer metre: attics keep exactly this — the first drafts.
- **Spanish — el ático.** Antonio Machado, "Retrato" (Campos de Castilla,
  1917): "Mi infancia son recuerdos de un patio de Sevilla, / y un huerto
  claro donde madura el limonero." — My childhood is memories of a patio in
  Seville and a bright orchard where the lemon tree ripens: the attic of a
  life, in two lines.
- **English — the attic.** Emily Dickinson, "I dwell in Possibility" (c.
  1862): "I dwell in Possibility – / A fairer House than Prose – / More
  numerous of Windows – / Superior – for Doors – / Of Chambers as the Cedars
  – / Impregnable of eye – / And for an everlasting Roof / The Gambrels of the
  Sky –" — A house built of poems, with cedar chambers and the sky for a
  roof: what every attic aspires to be.

## The basement (el sòtan / el sótano / the basement)

And there is no basement either — no cellar, no roots, no dark rooms under
the front desk. The building sits on the street it was built on. Still, a
basement is where a house tells the truth: foundations, timber, what was
stored and never thrown away. Three verses stand in for the stairs that go
nowhere:

- **Catalan — el sòtan.** Joan Salvat-Papasseit, "Nocturn per a acordió"
  (Óssa menor, 1925): "Vosaltres no sabeu / què és / guardar fusta al moll:
  / però jo he vist la pluja / a barrals / sobre els bots, / i dessota els
  taulons arraulir-se el preu fet de l'angoixa." — You don't know what it is
  to guard timber at the wharf: under the planks, cowering, the price made
  of anguish. The poet was a night watchman at Barcelona's timber wharf;
  this is the best basement in Catalan, and it is made of a dock.
- **Spanish — el sótano.** Federico García Lorca, "Romance de la pena negra"
  (1928): "¡Oh pena de los gitanos! / Pena limpia y siempre sola. / ¡Oh pena
  de cauce oculto / y madrugada remota!" — Oh the sorrow of the gypsies, clean
  and always alone: a sorrow of hidden channels and remote dawns. A hidden
  channel running under a house is, structurally, a basement.
- **English — the basement.** William Blake, "The Tyger" (Songs of Experience,
  1794): "Tyger, tyger, burning bright / In the forests of the night: / What
  immortal hand or eye, / Could frame thy fearful symmetry? / In what distant
  deeps or skies, / Burnt the fire of thine eyes?" — Distant deeps: the fires
  under the building. Blake asks who dared frame it; Jordi's answer is a test
  suite.

## How to use this floor plan

If a visitor asks what is on a real floor, answer from the floor list above
(citing this file). If a visitor asks about the roof, the attic or the
basement — or in Catalan "el terrat", "les golfes", "el sòtan"; in Spanish "la
azotea", "el ático", "el sótano" — say plainly that those floors were never
built, and then recite the verse for that floor in the language the visitor
speaks, with its attribution. The poetry is building lore, public domain, on
file here so I can quote it honestly rather than from memory I don't have.
